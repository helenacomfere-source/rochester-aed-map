If someone collapses

Sudden cardiac arrest is survivable, and what bystanders do in the first few minutes matters more than anything that happens later.

This page is not a substitute for training

Reading this is not the same as being certified. Certification courses are offered by the American Red Cross, the American Heart Association, and local hospitals. If you are with someone right now who is unresponsive, call 911 and follow the dispatcher's instructions.

The steps

  1. Check for a response Tap their shoulders and shout. If they do not respond and are not breathing normally, treat it as cardiac arrest. Slow, irregular gasping — called agonal breathing — is not normal breathing. It is a common sign of cardiac arrest, and mistaking it for breathing is one of the main reasons bystanders delay starting CPR. Source: AHA, Emergency Treatment of Cardiac Arrest
  2. Call 911 and send for an AED Put the phone on speaker. Point at a specific person and give them one job: "You — go get the AED." Vague requests to a crowd tend to go unanswered.
  3. Start chest compressions Push hard and fast in the center of the chest — at least 2 inches deep for an adult, and no more than 2.4 inches — at a rate of 100 to 120 compressions per minute. Let the chest come all the way back up between compressions. If you are untrained, hands-only CPR without rescue breaths is what the American Heart Association recommends. Source: AHA, High-Quality CPR
  4. Use the AED as soon as it arrives Turn it on and follow the spoken prompts. It will not deliver a shock unless one is needed, so you cannot hurt someone by attaching it to a person who does not need it.
  5. Keep going until help takes over Resume compressions right after any shock. Continue until EMS arrives or the person starts breathing normally on their own.

Common questions

Can I get in trouble for helping?

Minnesota's Good Samaritan law protects people who give reasonable assistance at the scene of an emergency in good faith. Fear of liability should not stop you from acting. Source: Minnesota Statutes § 604A.01. General information, not legal advice.

What if I have never used an AED?

AEDs are designed for untrained bystanders. Once turned on, the device gives spoken instructions and analyzes the heart rhythm itself — it will only advise a shock if the rhythm is one a shock can treat. Doing nothing is far more dangerous than using one imperfectly. Source: American Red Cross, What Is an AED

Why does verification matter on this map?

An AED that has been moved, locked away, or left with a dead battery is not useful in an emergency. That is why every record here shows when it was last confirmed in person, and why records we have not checked recently are labeled as out of date rather than quietly shown as reliable.

Why this matters for young athletes

Sudden cardiac arrest is a leading cause of death in young athletes. Roughly 2,000 people under 25 in the United States die of sudden cardiac arrest each year, and in athletes it often happens during practice or competition — settings where a bystander is always nearby and an AED is often only a short run away, if someone knows where it is. Knowing the location in advance is the part that has to happen before the emergency, not during it. Source: American Academy of Pediatrics, Sudden Cardiac Arrest in Young People
